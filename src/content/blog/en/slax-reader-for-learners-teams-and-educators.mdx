We recently talked with a corporate training organization in London about a familiar problem: trainers recommend two or three readings to learners in every session, but once the course ends, those links are scattered across emails and chat threads, and they're hard to find again.

Good content isn't the problem. What's missing is a place where it can stay, be found, and be shared again. Here is how Slax Reader works in three situations.

## 1. If you are learning a new field

You're working your way through a new field, and every day you find valuable reading in links from colleagues, newsletters and social media.

- **Save now, read later.** When you find a good article, save it to Slax Reader and read it later in a clean, focused view.
- **Highlight and comment as you read.** When you come back, you see not just the text, but what you were thinking at the time.
- **Learn in public.** Set up an account just for this subject and star the articles worth recommending. They're added to that account's collection automatically. Classmates and people you know online can subscribe, read along with you, and see your highlights and comments. When a friend asks "what should I read to get started?", one collection link is enough.

## 2. If you are a team that needs to share knowledge

Bosses and colleagues often share links in Slack, Teams or on LinkedIn. A few days later they're buried in the feed, and new hires have no way to find them. The London organization works the same way: they use Slack internally, the boss often shares articles in channels, and the team has built a library on "how we use AI internally."

- **Start with the founder making their reading list public.** The founder saves the references and sources they usually share in chat to Slax Reader and stars them, which adds them to the founder's collection. Once colleagues subscribe, they can see what the boss is reading, following and highlighting. It's a transparent way of learning together.
- **Copy existing knowledge management content over.** Documents, wikis and bookmarks can become a reading list in a collection, and you don't have to worry about the original web pages or links being taken down.
- **Help new people get up to speed.** A new hire subscribes to the founder's and colleagues' collections and sees the reading the team has built up over time.

## 3. If you are an education organization

An organization can work in two ways, or both at once.

### Option 1: Teachers own the reading list, and teachers and learners all take part

Each teacher stars course readings with their own account, which adds them to their collection. Learners subscribe and see the teacher's highlights and comments as they read. Learners can also set up their own accounts and share good reading with classmates.

Teachers don't need to spend extra time organizing links. Saving is part of their everyday reading, which saves time and effort. When the course ends, the list is still there, building a longer-lasting educational impact with every cohort.

### Option 2: The organization has its own account and builds a reading library that keeps growing

The organization sets up an official account and keeps adding high-quality content from across the internet that it considers worthwhile to that account's collection.

- **Reading material is a value-added asset for learners.** The two or three readings a trainer or industry expert provides each time can be collected in the organization's collection. They don't belong to a single course run. They become a value-added asset for learners that keeps growing and building up.
- **A fallback when experts prefer not to share publicly.** The organization can give learners the reading lists built up from previous courses first.

Internally, the library keeps enriching the organization's knowledge, and trainers and staff can look things up or add to it at any time. Externally, it enriches the value-added services the organization offers learners and clients, and gives industry experts' insights a lasting, visible home.
