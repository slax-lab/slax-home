It was a Sunday morning, and I was walking back from getting coffee when a friend sent me an article with a line: “I'm sure you'd love to read this.”

I opened it at the next crossing. It was good. Annoyingly good, actually. The kind of article where you read the first four paragraphs and immediately start thinking about three other things you’re working on. I wanted to keep reading, but I was heading to the next destination, and the article was several thousand words long.

So I did what I had done hundreds of times before.

I left the tab open.

I would read it later.

If you spend much of your life researching, writing, reporting, or simply reading things on the internet, you probably know what “later” means.

Later means the article joins 36 other open tabs on your phone. Later means you eventually close Safari because you are tired of looking at them. Later means you vaguely remember reading something useful about a subject three weeks from now, but you cannot remember the headline, the author, or where you found it.

I have lost an embarrassing number of good sources this way.

As a former journalist in tech and a passionate lifelong learner, I work with information for a living, and my days are full of things I am not ready to read yet. Someone sent me a link on IM. I discover an interesting research paper while looking for something completely unrelated. There is an interesting thread on X, or a technical explanation buried inside a website I may never visit again. The problem is that discovery and attention rarely happen at the same time.

There is the obvious problem: “I found something useful and don’t have time to read it.” But underneath that are more specific problems. How do I organize news and my articles? Should I save an online article before it disappears? How do I find the page I saved months ago? How do I save webpages from my phone and read them on my laptop? How do I keep track of sources? How do I save a Twitter / X thread for research? Can AI summarize all these articles without any of my efforts so that I could quickly decide how much I would spend in digesting it?

Those sound like separate questions. In my daily work, they are usually different versions of the same question:

**How do I stop useful information from disappearing from my life?**

Browser bookmarks were my first answer years ago. The problem with bookmarks is that saving is easy and finding is terrible. My bookmark folders slowly became a digital attic. I had folders called “Research,” “Ideas,” “Important,” “Writing,” “AI,” and, for reasons I no longer understand, another folder called “Research 2.” I could save almost anything. I could find almost nothing. Keeping tabs open was worse. At one point I had 147 tabs open across my phone and laptop. Some were articles I genuinely intended to read. Others were sources for my last presentation. Some had been open so long that closing them felt weirdly like destroying historical records.

Then I tried dedicated tools: Pocket, Instapaper, Readwise Reader, Omnivore, Pinboard, notes apps, databases, and various combinations of all of the above. But at some point, they all had some annoying details, such as capture issues, unanswered bug reports, high fees, etc. I got tired of going back to platforms that reminded me of those annoyances and continuously asked me to pay. After a while, I noticed something else: every tool eventually became its own kind of information dump.

One day, a colleague and I were talking about a topic I was recently researching. She said, “I think I read something about this on X,” and sent me a link.

I opened it expecting another thread. Instead, I got a beautifully clean version of a post that didn’t look like the X website. Just the content, with her comment on it. The original source was still right there. I clicked it and landed on the original X page. I started to explore the website called Slax Reader.

I started trying it with my own research. After a while, three things made it stick.

## I can keep a clean copy without losing the original source

For research, provenance matters.

If I save an X thread, an article, or a news website, I don't want the reader app to separate the content from its source. I need to know where it came from, who published it, and how to get back to the original.

Slax Reader gives me the cleaner reading experience I want while keeping the original link easy to reach. It gathers everyday research pages at one place.

I can capture something when I find it, read it without the surrounding clutter, and still jump back to the original source to cite. And when a colleague asks, “Where did you get this?”, I can share what I saved in my collection instead of hunting through browser history or sending a screenshot with no context. My workflow has gradually become very simple: **capture it, read it, trace it, share it.**

I spend much less time trying to remember where something came from.

## My phone, laptop, and browsers finally share the same memory

Valuable content does not happen exclusively at a desk, but Slax Reader syncs across my devices.

I might discover an article on my laptop during work, read half of it on my phone later, and return to the same material when I am preparing a presentation the next morning. Sometimes I find something while commuting. Sometimes I could even use offline mode at airports.

I don't need to think, “Wait, where did I see it or save it, on my phone or on my laptop?”

With Slax Reader, my library is the library. It works across the web, browser extension, and native mobile app and even a telegram bot. I can save something in one place and return to it somewhere else.

## I finally brought my scattered research into one place

The third reason was the one I didn't expect to care about so much. I didn't have to start over.

After years of trying different apps, tools, and bookmark managers, I already had a lot of material scattered across different services. Some of it was recent. Some went back years.

Starting another app from zero would have meant creating yet another information island. Slax Reader supports importing existing bookmarks from services including Pocket, Omnivore, Readwise Reader, Instapaper, and Pinboard. So instead of treating my previous experiments as wasted time, I could bring those libraries together. And all those things were done at no cost. Just a free tool that boosts my productivity and efficiency, and provides reliable, responsive user support.

That is probably the biggest change Slax Reader has made to the way I work. It probably hasn't made me read everything I save. I still save too much. I'm probably never the most organized writer ever.

But I have no need to be. When I find something useful now, I send it to Slax Reader. When I need it again, I know where to look. If I need to check the source, the original link is there. If I need to share it with a colleague or friend, I get it in one click with a clean design. If I saved it on another device, it still follows me. And the material I collected in previous tools doesn't have to disappear simply because my workflow changed.

For me, that is what a good productivity tool does.
