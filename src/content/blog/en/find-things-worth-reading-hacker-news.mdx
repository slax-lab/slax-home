The internet is very good at rewarding what's new.

New articles, new tools, new arguments. What was on the front page yesterday goes unread today, and whether it kept helping anyone stops being the point.

But useful and new have never been the same thing.

A Hacker News (HN) user once wrote in a comment:

> I want to share the most wonderful tutorial on how to build hexagonal grids for games and things

The link was a Red Blob Games tutorial on hex grids. What drew me in wasn't just the phrase "most wonderful." It was the time behind it.

From 2013 to 2026, 37 HN users mentioned that tutorial, 40 times in all, across 13 different years.

One link, handed forward by different people in different discussions. It was never continuously popular. It was simply needed, again and again.

That's why we built [HackerLinks, a library of resources from long-running Hacker News discussions](https://slax.com/reader/hackerlinks/en/).

The idea was inspired by Andrej Karpathy's [HackerTube](https://hackertube.org/). Thank you, Andrej. HackerTube gathers the YouTube videos people share in HN comments. HackerLinks takes the same approach to many more kinds of pages.

## What's still worth keeping after the attention moves on

Home feeds and recommendation streams keep telling us what we have to know right now. New content is what they have most of. What they lack is a reason for old content to surface again.

Yet the problem someone runs into today may have been answered with care ten years ago. A piece of documentation, a long essay, or an interactive tutorial doesn't lose its ability to help because the publish date is old.

HN comments preserve another kind of time.

Someone asks a question now. Someone else reaches for an article they read years ago. A tool gets named by a person who used it, then named again in a later discussion by someone else. One recommendation can be a passing mood. Recommendations that cross years and come from different people carry a different weight.

We didn't want to turn that weight into a quality score. We'd rather read it as a plain signal: someone used this, and later, in another discussion, someone else recommended it again.

## Three starting points across the years

### 1. A good tutorial gives thinking somewhere to begin

Hex grids sound like a niche problem. Then you run into one, and coordinate systems, distance, range, pathfinding, and map storage are all in the way at once.

What makes the [Red Blob Games hex grid guide](https://slax.com/reader/hackerlinks/en/resources/r_3e07602e964a011331e77ca5bf3164dd/) unusual is that it doesn't stop at handing you formulas. It puts the relationships in front of you as interactive diagrams. You change something and watch one choice reshape the next.

The guide is mostly pseudocode. The implementation guide carries code in C++, JavaScript, C#, Python, Java, and TypeScript.

A good tutorial peels the complexity back, layer by layer, until the reader is willing to try.

### 2. Some writing is worth slowing down for

Michael Nielsen's essay [on understanding a piece of mathematics through spaced repetition](https://slax.com/reader/hackerlinks/en/resources/r_b7182c13b2cd50c4f4c7b3b9928c1b7c/) was published in 2019.

We like to picture understanding as a moment: you didn't get it, then you did. Nielsen won't accept that tidy version. He's writing about deepening comprehension over time, and he demonstrates the whole process on a single linear algebra theorem.

The essay leans on Anki, a flashcard program, but the software isn't the point. Nielsen doesn't promise to teach you mathematics in ten minutes. He admits the method takes time, and that it may not suit people with different backgrounds. That honesty is what earns trust: understanding deeply has a cost.

Between 2019 and 2026, 18 HN users mentioned the essay, 19 times. The question it leaves behind outlasts the tool: finishing a page and understanding it are not the same event.

### 3. Old essays can be argued with

The third example is less warm. It matters just as much.

Paul Graham's [essay on why startups cluster in America](https://slax.com/reader/hackerlinks/en/resources/r_81f3f2057d3a530bceb6a39e803866ae/) was published in 2006. The argument runs through immigration, the economy, politics, and universities.

From 2007 to 2025, 21 HN users mentioned it, 29 times.

That doesn't mean its conclusions still hold. Read it in the context of 2006, the year it was written. You can follow how the author explains where startups cluster, and you can test which judgments survived the twenty years since.

Keeping the old essay around lets us return to the scene: which judgments have changed, and which questions are still open.

## Comments are evidence, not answers

HackerLinks works from the public Hacker News archive.

Every resource in the public catalog has been mentioned by at least 3 different users in 3 different months. The system normalizes addresses, merges the URL variants that point at the same resource, and sorts comments by intent: recommendation, neutral citation, criticism.

Where the page can be read, the system writes the summary, content type, and topics from the page text together with the comment context. Every detail page keeps one short HN quote and a link to the original comment. Where the source can be read, it also keeps a short excerpt as evidence.

Summaries and inclusion notes are generated automatically, and they can be wrong. Pages change. Old writing goes stale. Commenters misjudge things. HackerLinks doesn't dress any of this up as a hand-picked best-of list. The link to the original and the link to the comment stay on the page, so readers can go past our summary and check for themselves. That matters more than any "curated" badge.

## A second chance for old pages to be found

Today HackerLinks holds 14,049 resources, with mentions running from 2007 to 2026.

Pouring fourteen thousand resources in front of a reader at once would only recreate the overload. So the [weekly discovery page](https://slax.com/reader/hackerlinks/en/) cycles through 100 items drawn from resources with long-running recommendations.

"Weekly" doesn't mean only what arrived this week. It means letting old resources come back into view.

If you already know what you're after, open the [full catalog](https://slax.com/reader/hackerlinks/en/all/) and browse from 15 topics: programming, science, business, math, and more. When something catches your eye, you can save it for later. Saved items stay in your current browser and are never uploaded. When you want to keep something across devices, save the original in [Slax Reader](https://r.slax.com/).
